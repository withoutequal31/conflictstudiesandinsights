01

Russia–Ukraine

Ukraine widens pressure on Russian logistics beyond the Black Sea. Ukrainian attacks on the Kerch Bridge and Russian vessels in Libya and the Gulf of Finland indicate a widening campaign against Moscow’s supply network. These strikes increase logistical pressure and demonstrate Ukraine’s extended reach, but do not yet establish a decisive shift in the ground campaign. Source: Newswav, 23 Nov

02

Russia–Europe tensions

Missile incursions increase pressure on NATO’s eastern-flank defences. Polish prosecutors identified three objects that entered Poland on 20 November as Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles, revising earlier reports of two missiles and a drone. The incident prompted NATO consultations and Polish requests for stronger air defences. Assessment: The incursions heighten escalation risks and pressure NATO to reinforce protection without entering the war directly; deliberate Russian targeting of Poland remains unconfirmed. Source: Reuters, 22 Nov

Other conflicts · US–Israel–Iran, Israel–Lebanon, Israel–Gaza
03

US–Israel–Iran

Blockade-driven stalemate risks renewed strikes. As of 23 November, the theatre remains locked in a coercive stalemate: Washington is using its naval blockade to pressure Tehran into nuclear concessions, while Iran demands blockade relief, restored oil sales and access to frozen assets before substantive negotiations. Iran’s latest warning that it would use “all available tools” against the blockade indicates continued resistance rather than convergence toward an agreement. Source: Reuters, 23 Nov

04

Israel–Lebanon

Deeper Israeli operations threaten to widen fighting despite ceasefire. Netanyahu announced further advances into Lebanon on 22 November, while Israel reported capturing Beaufort Castle and a strategic ridge following intensified Hezbollah fire into northern Israel. Assessment: The ceasefire is failing to contain reciprocal escalation, with Israeli ground advances and Hezbollah drone attacks sustaining pressure on both sides. These developments indicate expanding hostilities, but do not yet establish an unrestricted return to full-scale war. Source: Dawn

05

Israel–Gaza

Ceasefire limits major fighting but leaves the underlying conflict unresolved. Israeli strikes continued on 23 November, killing at least three people, including a UNRWA worker, according to the agency and local health officials. Earlier Israeli operations west of the Yellow Line also demonstrated continued military action beyond areas under its control. Assessment: The ceasefire has constrained major fighting without resolving Hamas’s disarmament or securing a durable halt to attacks, leaving Gaza vulnerable to recurring raids, civilian casualties and renewed escalation. Source: Reuters, 23 Nov

Manoeuvre & Sustainment

Operation Vivaldi and the “Four Seasons” of integrated assault

Russia–Ukraine, eastern “fortress belt” · 11–24 Nov 2026

Preliminary · Medium confidence Medium for the overall sequence and effects, from one Reuters report drawing on Ukraine’s Third Army Corps. Lower for repeatability, scale and sustainment burden, which were not reported.

Swipe for the next panel →

LessonEach phase lowered the risk of the next: blind the enemy, send machines in first, move infantry under electronic-warfare cover, then hold the positions gained. The sequence, not any single platform, made the difference.

Questions to askWhich steps of this sequence could units rehearse now, and which depend on drone, robot and electronic-warfare support they may not have?

Function
Manoeuvre, with links to sustainment
Learning status
Preliminary but highly relevant operational observation. Confidence is medium for the broad sequence and effects described by Reuters; lower for exact repeatability, scale and sustainment burden across other sectors.
QuestionPreliminary findings
Operating problemUkraine needed to break up part of a Russian pincer advance in an environment where the frontline kill zone was saturated with drones, mines and constant observation, making conventional exposed movement costly. Source: Reuters, 18 Nov
Why “Four Seasons” mattersReuters reported that the operation’s name referred to its four phases, in a nod to Vivaldi’s Four Seasons concertos. Those phases are useful not as a musical metaphor, but as an operational sequence explaining how Ukraine pushed Russian troops back from selected positions.
Season 1 — Blind and shape the battlefieldUkraine first attacked the Russian system before the close assault. Mid-range strike drones hit supply routes and command posts well behind the front, while air-defence units targeted Russian reconnaissance drones to degrade Moscow’s view of Ukrainian preparations. This mattered because it reduced Russia’s ability to see the assault assembling and coordinate its own response in time.
Season 2 — Open routes and put unmanned systems forward firstUkraine then used remotely controlled ground vehicles to attack positions, carry explosives and clear mines, including at least one reported case in which combat robots were dropped behind Russian lines by repurposed bomber drones. This phase reduced the need for soldiers to be the first exposed force entering mined or observed approaches.
Season 3 — Advance infantry under a protected domeAs assault troops moved forward in small groups, aerial drones attacked dugouts judged too dangerous to enter first, while the Third Army Corps said a “protective dome” of electronic warfare disrupted Russian drone operations overhead. Ukraine’s success here came from combining suppression, protection and small-unit movement rather than from a single decisive charge.
Season 4 — Consolidate local gains and break the pincer’s momentumReuters reported that the operation ended last month in a rare battlefield success for Ukraine: it did not recover large amounts of territory, but it helped thwart part of Russia’s pincer advance on eastern urban strongholds. The practical lesson is that success can mean denying the enemy’s operational design and pushing forces out of tactically important positions, even when territorial gains remain limited in absolute terms. Source: Reuters, 18 Nov · Reuters Connect
What remains unprovenThe reporting does not quantify platform losses, electronic-warfare consumption, maintenance demand, failed attacks or whether less-elite formations could reproduce the method at scale. It is therefore a strong operational case study, not yet proof of a broadly scalable template.
Preliminary observations

Operation Vivaldi shows that Ukraine succeeded not by replacing soldiers with machines, but by sequencing multiple functions so that each phase reduced the risk of the next. The four-phase design matters because it linked deep shaping, unmanned breach-and-strike activity, protected infantry advance and local consolidation into one operating concept.

Validation question

Which parts of this four-phase sequence are essential for success, and which are contingent on unit quality, Russian weakness in that sector or available electronic-warfare density?

Sense & Strike

Resilient strike networks: Communications are part of the weapon

Russia–Ukraine, Kerch Bridge · 11–24 Nov 2026

Preliminary · Medium confidence Medium for the reported relay-drone method, from the Financial Times via Ars Technica. Its contribution to the strike’s success has not been independently verified.

Swipe for the next panel →

LessonA strike capability is only as usable as its network. Ukraine reportedly kept control of long-range drones by relaying commands through mesh radios on other drones when satellite access was restricted.

Questions to askDo trials test whether the mission still succeeds when the main communications service is unavailable, rather than only the weapon’s range under ideal connectivity?

Theatre
Russia–Ukraine
Reporting period
11–24 November 2026
Function
Sense and strike, with links to communications
Learning status
Preliminary observation · Medium confidence. The communications arrangement is reported through secondary coverage citing the Financial Times; its independent contribution to strike success remains unverified.
QuestionPreliminary findings
Operating problemLong-range weapons may remain physically capable of reaching a target while losing access to the communications service supporting their employment. Reported restrictions on satellite connectivity created this dependency problem for Ukrainian systems. The restrictions were disputed by SpaceX, so their precise application should not be treated as settled. Source: Ars Technica, 18 Nov
Ukraine’s adaptationReporting on the 15–16 November Kerch Bridge strikes described drones carrying mesh radios that relayed communications between operators and strike systems. This reportedly provided an alternative to the satellite service normally used, illustrating adaptation of the supporting network rather than simply development of a longer-range weapon. Source: Financial Times · Ars Technica, 18 Nov
What enabled the reported resultThe communications arrangement formed one part of a wider operation that reportedly included preparatory attacks against Russian defensive systems and coordinated missile and drone employment. The available evidence does not isolate the mesh network as the decisive factor; it describes an enabling component within a combined operation.
What remains unprovenThe reviewed report does not quantify network availability, resistance to interference, relay losses or performance after individual nodes failed. It therefore supports the reported use of an alternative communications architecture, not proof of uninterrupted control in a heavily contested electromagnetic environment.
Preliminary observations

A strike capability’s usable reach depends on its supporting network, not weapon range alone. The analytical implication is to evaluate sensing, communications, control and weapons as a complete mission system — including whether the mission remains feasible when a principal service is restricted or unavailable.

An alternative communications path should not automatically be labelled resilient. Its value must be demonstrated through mission completion under degraded conditions, accounting for additional equipment, operator workload and dependence on the alternative network.

To consider
  • Capability evaluation: Do trials assess end-to-end mission completion when the primary communications service is unavailable, rather than demonstrate weapon range under ideal connectivity?
  • Operational dependency: Which sensing and strike functions depend on external services, permissions or infrastructure outside the formation’s control?
  • Degraded operation: Are fallback arrangements tested for their actual limitations, including workload, delays and safe loss-of-link behaviour?
